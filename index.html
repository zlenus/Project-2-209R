Proposition: U.S. States Don't Have Enough Abortion Clinics

FOR the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Inclusion of a trend line to imply cause and effect (-1): Adding a fitted line to the graphic, sways the reader to assume there is a relationship between the 2 variables. While there is a positive association between these elements as indicated by the fitted line, confounding variables have not been taken into account when working with the data.
  • Visual Coloring (-0.25): The usage of red throughout the scatterplot implies a negative tone. Isolating a section of data in red not only brings the viewers eyes to the highlighted information but mildly relays a sense of urgency. Using other tones like blue or gray, display like a generic statistical chart rather than a need for change.
  • Biased Title (-0.75): Title contains direct assumption to sway the reader. Rather than letting the reader decipher the graphic themselves, the title sways them to focus on a direct result. After reading this title, the viewer focuses on which states have residents leaving their home state in order to get care, rather than focusing on numerical values of how many residents fall under this value.

AGAINST the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Positive framing of data (1.5) : This graphic reframes the data shown in the for argument. If 8% of residents went out-of-state for care, we can reframe the data to emphasize the other 92% that received care within the state. Reframing the percentages who traveled to those that stayed to positively highlight the availability of care.
  • Usage of Whole Numbers and Percentages (0.5) : Rather than using only percentages as a comparative measurement, including the whole numbers in addition to percentages provides numerical weight. In addition, the inclusion of of both these types of quantitive measures strengths the data and allows for labels that highlight how impactful the data is.
  • Restricting the amount of data shown (1) : Cropping the amount of data also sways the viewer in favor of the presented argument. The trend line with the label that “96% stayed in-state” further pushes the idea that most patients got abortion care without having to leave their state.

Final Reflection

When starting this process, I was pushing to really try and explore how I can take the same variables and frame them in different aspects depending on the argument I was catering too. The concept was clear that the argument was to highlight the need for more abortion clinics and also dissuade the reader that the current amount is sufficient. While it took sometime to figure out how to do this while using the same variables, the idea of treating the values of the variables as complements of one another really helped establish each graphic. The FOR argument was the easiest to make as the data was already framed to highlight these differences. I was able to highlight the states that had these extreme values to make the scatterplot more dramatic; additionally the trend line also contributed to a narrative that care was lacking amongst all the states without specifying specific values. The AGAINST argument was a more difficult to frame but focusing the data to highlight that the majority of people received care within state was the best approach. Cropping the data to only include the top states with the highest average and including a median line conveyed a narrative that all 50 states enough abortion care for its residents. It’s hard to establish bounds as to what is too misleading and what isn’t. Over-manipulation of data can be deemed as misleading but also at times could be necessary in doing further analysis. Similarly, using a subsection of data can be seen as not generic enough while including outliers skews the data. I think the media should be persuasive without having to have an excess of labels/statements to sway the viewer but also the viewer should view these types of media with a critical eye and question what other confounding variables could be missing in those contexts.
